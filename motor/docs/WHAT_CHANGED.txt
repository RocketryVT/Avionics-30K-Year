WHAT CHANGED
============

Plain list of everything done on the tracking-and-gui branch, in the order
it was done. Nothing here is pushed.


1. THE GEAR SWITCH  (commit 7dd2fa9)
------------------------------------

- Before: both motors were counted as having only a 10:1 gearbox, so every
  angle was measured at the gearbox shaft and each motor had to be tested on
  its own.
- Now there is a switch. "gear off" counts the gearbox only. "gear on" also
  counts the belt's final gear pairs, so angles are measured at the antenna.
- Azimuth's pair is 32:14, elevation's is 20:14. Both come from last year's
  ground station firmware.
- Steps per degree: 44.444 with the gear off. With it on, 101.587 on azimuth
  and 63.492 on elevation.
- Speeds and accelerations are scaled down when the gear is on, so the motor
  is asked for exactly the same thing in both modes. Without that, a fast
  move in geared mode would ask the motor for about 690 rpm, past where its
  torque halves, and a stalled stepper loses position silently.
- Switching costs both axes their zero, because the drive train has
  physically changed. You have to zero again.
- The setting is saved in the Pico's flash, so a restart comes back in the
  same mode instead of quietly reverting.
- Results files gained a "gear" column, because an angle means nothing
  without knowing which gearing produced it.
- Still unchecked on this machine: nobody has counted azimuth's 32 teeth,
  and elevation's 0-180 travel has not been measured on the assembled
  antenna. Both are one-line edits in motor_config.hpp.


2. A WINDOW THAT WATCHES  (commit 4bb62ad)
------------------------------------------

- New: "py -m gs.gui" opens a window showing what the Pico is doing.
- Shows: which port and which build, how long it has been up, the heartbeat,
  the gearing and what it makes a degree worth, position in degrees and in
  steps, a large red NOT ZEROED warning, the travel and speed limits, and
  every line going to and from the Pico.
- Read-only at this stage. It could not command anything.
- Split into two files on purpose. gui_worker.py talks to the Pico on its own
  thread; gui.py only draws. Without that split the window would freeze for
  up to a minute every time a move ran.
- Nothing on screen is remembered. Every number shown is what the Pico last
  said, so the window can never disagree with the machine.
- Found and fixed a bug in the existing code: if the USB cable was pulled
  while a command was being sent, the error came out as a type nothing was
  prepared for, and the text prompt would have crashed with a traceback
  instead of printing a message. This fixes the prompt too, not just the
  window.


3. MODES 1 AND 2 IN THE WINDOW  (commit 90fd696)
------------------------------------------------

- The window can now drive the machine. No firmware change was needed - this
  uses commands that already existed.
- Three modes, picked with radio buttons:
    1. independent - gear off, one axis at a time
    2. combined    - gear on, both axes in one command
    3. track       - added later, in step 7
- Picking a mode sets the gearing for you. Because that costs both axes their
  zero, it asks first and puts the button back if you say no.
- Controls added: zero (both or one axis), a move per mode, acceleration and
  travel editable per axis with apply/save/defaults, and STOP and E-STOP
  across the bottom. Escape is the ramped stop - the key that is easy to hit
  by accident is the one that does not lose the zero.
- Stop does not wait its turn. A move holds the connection for as long as it
  runs, so a queued stop would arrive after the move it was meant to cut
  short. It now reaches a running move within a fifth of a second.
- Closing the window while an axis is turning stops it.
- Everything except the two stops is disabled while a command is running, and
  a move is disabled until the axes are zeroed.
- Apply only sends the settings that actually changed.
- Three bugs the tests caught:
    - Two different methods were both called "stop", so the one meant to shut
      the worker thread down was silently replaced and the thread never
      exited.
    - A bad number typed into a box killed the background thread, leaving a
      window that had quietly stopped updating.
    - Two places had to be kept in step by hand to know the current state.
      Now there is one.
- Settings boxes keep what you have half typed instead of losing it to the
  next refresh.


4. THE CHASE MATHS  (commit 99ac50b)
------------------------------------

- Arithmetic only. Nothing used it yet. Done on its own so the part most
  likely to misbehave could be run thousands of times at a desk first.
- The problem it solves: an ordinary move works out a whole speed plan up
  front and follows it to the last step. If the target changes halfway
  through there is nothing to update.
- The new way carries its speed instead and asks one question over and over:
  how far off am I, and how fast should I be going now?
- Two things cap the answer. The axis's speed limit, and how fast it could
  still brake from. The second is what matters - go faster than that and it
  physically cannot stop in time, so it sails past, comes back, sails past
  again, and hunts forever.
- Those two hand over to each other automatically, so for a target that is
  not moving this produces the same motion an ordinary move would, without
  ever planning it.
- Known and written down rather than fixed: it follows a moving target at a
  steady distance behind, equal to the rate squared divided by twice the
  acceleration. About 3.4 degrees at 20 deg/s on azimuth, 13.6 at 40 deg/s.
  Nothing against an antenna beam about 50 degrees wide, but it grows with
  the square of the rate.
- New setting: the deadband, meaning how close to a target counts as being
  there. Half a degree by default.


5. "GOTO"  (commit ef767a6)
---------------------------

- "move" turns BY an amount and stays that way, because the bench procedure
  is "+45, read the protractor, -45, read it again".
- There was no way at all to say "point at 45". You had to read the position
  and do the subtraction yourself.
- New: "goto az 5 45" turns TO 45 degrees, wherever it started. Works for
  both axes at once too. In the window, every move panel now has a "turn by"
  button and a "go to" button against one angle box.
- The subtraction happens on the Pico, because the step count is the only
  record of where the axis is and it lives there. The laptop's copy can be up
  to half a second old.
- Rounding happens once. Twenty "goto"s in a row land on the nearest step to
  where you asked, not twenty roundings away from it.
- Asking "goto" for the angle it is already at is refused.


6. THE PICO CAN CHASE A TARGET  (commit fcf9b06)
------------------------------------------------

- The biggest firmware change. The chase maths from step 4 is now wired up,
  so the motor can be told "point here" over and over and will keep closing
  the gap by itself.
- New commands: "track on" / "track off" to arm and disarm, and TARGET to say
  where to point. TARGET is sent by a program, not typed.
- A TARGET does nothing unless tracking is armed, so a stray message cannot
  move anything.
- A target outside an axis's travel is held at the limit instead of refused.
  This is deliberately different from how a move is treated. A move is a
  finite thing somebody typed, so refusing it whole is right. A target
  arrives several times a second from a rocket that does not know where the
  gimbal can point - refusing would leave the antenna stuck with no way back,
  while holding at the limit keeps it as close as it can get and picks the
  rocket up again by itself.
- A watchdog. If no target arrives for about a second and a half, both axes
  ramp to rest and stay there. That is what a radio dropout or a closed
  laptop looks like from the Pico's side, and it is what makes holding at a
  limit safe rather than reckless.
- Stop, emergency stop and a gear change all end tracking. The gear change
  has to, because a degree means something different afterwards.
- Telemetry that never waits. The firmware's rule was that nothing is printed
  while a motor is turning, because writing to USB can block and that would
  stall the motor part way round. Under tracking the motor is nearly always
  turning, so that rule would mean no telemetry at all. It now asks whether
  there is room for a whole line and drops the line if not. A dropped line
  costs a moment of staleness; waiting would cost a stalled motor.
- The deadband is now adjustable without reflashing - a command at the prompt
  and a box in the window. Raise it if the motors chatter, lower it for
  tighter pointing.
- Could not be checked without hardware: whether that non-blocking write
  really never blocks on a real Pico with a laptop that has stopped reading.
  First thing to try at the bench.


7. WHERE TARGETS COME FROM  (commit 60c8c96)
--------------------------------------------

- Laptop only. No firmware change.
- Three sources, one interface:
    manual     a direction you type. Takes the rocket, the radio and the
               geometry out of the picture, so what is left under test is the
               chase loop. Use this first on real hardware.
    simulated  a rehearsal. Either a worked-out flight (straight up from a
               pad 300 m away to about 30,000 ft, then down under a
               parachute) or a recorded flight replayed from a file.
    live       where the lat/lon/alt to az/el module plugs in when it is
               ready. Hand it a function and it works.
- New: "py -m gs.track --sim --dry-run" prints the angles a flight would ask
  for, with no Pico and no motors, and reports the fastest elevation sweep in
  it - about 30 degrees a second in the first two seconds, because close to
  the pad a little altitude is a lot of angle. That is the number to compare
  against an axis's speed limit.
- The same command shows elevation reaching 87.6 degrees, which is almost
  straight overhead. An az/el mount cannot track through directly overhead at
  any gear ratio, so that is worth seeing in a rehearsal.
- A flight log is written as it happens: one row per target sent, with the
  direction asked for, the one the Pico is using, where it has got to, how
  far off it is and how fast it is turning. The first two differ when a
  target is out of reach, which one column would have hidden.
- What your partner's module should hand over: absolute angles, not changes.
  There is a fallback that adds up changes if it has to, and its notes say
  why it is the worse option.
- Two bugs the tests caught:
    - A refused arm left a callback hooked to a dead loop forever, so the
      text prompt's own output would have stayed broken after any failed
      flight.
    - Working out "is this target out of reach" took two wrong answers before
      the obvious one. The laptop already knows what it asked for, so it just
      checks that against the limits.


8. MODE 3 IN THE WINDOW, AND BETTER HELP  (this commit)
-------------------------------------------------------

- The third mode is now in the window. Pick a source (a direction you type,
  or a simulated flight), set how many targets a second, then ARM.
- Arming is a separate click from choosing the mode, and it asks first,
  because this is the only mode where the motors move without anybody
  pressing anything.
- While tracking it shows, for each axis: the target, where it actually is,
  how far off it is, and how fast it is turning. Plus how many targets have
  been sent.
- If a target is out of reach it says so plainly, so a stubborn error reads as
  "the rocket is outside what the mount can do" rather than a fault.
- If the Pico's watchdog gives up on its own, the window follows it and says
  what happened.
- While tracking, the window stops asking its usual questions, because the
  Pico refuses them all while a motor turns. The telemetry stream carries the
  readings instead.
- Disarm stays available at all times, as do STOP and E-STOP. Switching modes
  is locked while tracking.
- The commands panel is now the long form: for every command, what it does
  AND when you would reach for it. That second part is the bit that is hard
  to work out from a list.
- A test holds that reference against the prompt's real command list, so it
  cannot drift and no command can be added without a line saying when to use
  it.
- Found while testing: the direction boxes were disabled until tracking was
  armed, which is backwards - you type where to point and then arm.

- Added a box to type commands into, under the reference. Before this the
  window told you about commands with nowhere to type them, and anything the
  buttons did not cover meant closing the window and opening the text prompt
  instead (only one program can hold the serial port at a time).
- It runs the line through the text prompt's own code, so a command means
  exactly the same thing in both places - same parsing, same error messages,
  same results file. The things the buttons do not cover now work in the
  window: sweep, random, true, note, rows, pin.
- Up and down arrows walk back through what you have typed.
- A typed move can be stopped by the STOP button, which took a small change:
  the window's wait for a move to finish is the interruptible one, so a typed
  "move az 1 360" cannot hold everything up for six minutes with the stop
  unable to reach it.
- "stop" and "estop" typed in the box skip the queue the same way the buttons
  do, since a stop waiting behind the move it is meant to cut short would be
  no stop at all.
- The window now takes --log as well, so the typed true/note/rows have a
  results file to write to.


9. AT THE BENCH, 25 OCT 2026
----------------------------

- Elevation direction. A 180 degree move looked like it went the wrong way,
  so the direction setting was flipped. A test with small 10 degree moves
  then showed the original setting was right after all, so it was flipped
  back. Lesson: judge direction with small moves. A half turn ends in the
  same place whichever way it goes, so it cannot tell you the direction.
- Azimuth direction: "move az" turned the right way. Not yet checked in the
  other gear mode, where the azimuth gears could reverse it.
- Elevation can now go down to -25 degrees, in both gear modes. This needed a
  code change because the prompt can only ever narrow the travel, never widen
  it - with no limit switches, widening is meant to be done on purpose.
- Azimuth now runs from -180 to +180 degrees, in both gear modes, instead of
  0 to 360.
- There is no wrap-around. "goto az 5 179" from -179 turns 358 degrees the
  long way through 0, never the 2 degree short cut across 180. Anything
  cabled to the azimuth is never wound more than one turn.
- Tracking had to change to match. Positions come in as compass bearings, 0
  to 360. A bearing of 270 would have been held at the +180 limit, pointing
  90 degrees the wrong way. Bearings are now converted first, so 270 becomes
  -90. This assumes the azimuth was zeroed facing north.
- The catch: due south is now the seam. A rocket drifting across due south
  goes from +179.9 to -179.9, and the antenna turns a full circle the other
  way to follow it. The rehearsal flight does this about 8 minutes in.
- Version stamps: the firmware only picks up a new version number when the
  build is set up from scratch, so two builds of different code reported the
  same version and nobody could tell which was on the Pico. Builds now
  commit first and set up fresh, so each one reports its own version.


HOW IT WAS CHECKED
==================

- The Pico firmware builds from scratch with no warnings, and produces a
  .uf2 ready to flash.
- 302 checks on the firmware maths, run on a PC rather than a Pico: steps per
  degree in both gearings, the travel and speed limits, the saved-settings
  record, absolute moves, and the chase loop driven thousands of times
  against still targets, moving targets, targets it cannot catch, and a
  stalled loop.
- 480 checks on the laptop software, including the window built and driven
  with no screen attached, and a pretend Pico standing in for the real one.
- None of it has been run on the actual hardware. That is the next step.


WHAT TO DO AT THE BENCH
=======================

In this order, because each step rules something out:

1. Flash it. Check the startup text says the gearing and deadband you expect.
2. Mode 1, gear off, one axis. Turn 45 degrees and check a protractor reads
   45.000.
3. Fit the belt. Mode 2, gear on. Check both axes again - if a move comes out
   1.43 or 2.29 times too far, the gearing is the wrong way round.
4. Mode 3 with a typed direction. Watch it slew and settle.
   Then, WHILE IT IS STILL MOVING, change the numbers and press "point here"
   again. It should curve toward the new direction without ever coming to a
   stop. This is the thing modes 1 and 2 will not let you do: there, a
   second move while one is running is refused. In mode 3 new directions
   arriving mid-move is the normal case, and with a typed direction the same
   target is being re-sent five times a second anyway - pressing the button
   just changes the number being sent.
5. Pull the USB cable mid-track and check the motors ramp down rather than
   carrying on.
6. Then a simulated flight.


STILL OPEN
==========

- How often LoRa will deliver a position. This matters more than anything
  else left. At one a second the antenna could fall up to 40 degrees behind
  during the fast part of the climb, which is outside the beam. At five a
  second it is about 8 degrees and fine.
- Azimuth's 32 teeth have never been counted on this machine.
- Elevation's 0-180 travel has not been measured on the assembled antenna.
  Last year's system used -10 to +90, which is a much smaller range, and that
  is probably the right one.
- Whether elevation holds position under the antenna's weight. Last year used
  a sensor to correct it; we have no feedback at all.
- What to do near straight overhead, where an az/el mount cannot track. Most
  systems freeze the azimuth above some elevation and accept the error.
- The antenna's real beamwidth. The figure in last year's code is a
  placeholder; from the antenna type it is probably 40-50 degrees, which is
  forgiving.
